## Part 1 — The Problem

The owner of Brew and Byte gave my group a document that specified the main actors of the system and their responsibilities. Through our individual and group analyses, we scrutinized the given document, defined the actors, and established use cases for each of them.

---

## Part 2 — Your Design Decision

For our first group artifact (docs/week-03/group-artifact-1.md), we compiled our individual lists for the grouping of each requirement into the three categories: functional, non-functional, and neither. While reviewing our lists and updating the design, we found that we had a great deal of overlap that we had for the responsibilities and whether the system was responsible for maintaining them. The description of each element of the system as provided by the owner was categorized into a composite table of three columns (the requirement as listed in the document, which of the three groups the requirement fell into, and the disagreements we had, if any).

For our second group artifact (docs/week-03/group-artifact-2.md), we were each given one actor to build out into a use case diagram. Starting from the table we made in group-artifact-1.md, I used the barista's responsibilities thar we had deemed to be a functional part of the system and formatted them into use cases. Following that, I built a mermaid graph detailing the connection of each use case that connects to the barista's actions within the system. After the group reconvened, we combined out four graphs into a master graph in which all the responsibilities have been listed for each actor (two in the case of customer as two group members had been assigned the customer actor).

---

## Part 3 — How You Got There

During the table discussion, we argued that there were some points included by the owner that contradicted each other. The most glaring example of which was found between 2.7 (the customer's ability to update their order) and 3.4 (the assertion that orders cannot be upgraded after submission as barista's would be confused). This presented the largest glaring gap in the design as we noticed it as the first contradiction going down the list. Although this was an obvious question for the owner, other elements such as 4.6 (the owner's request to evaluate order time to completion) were a question that had us concerned about the specific wording we needed to come to a consensus for what the owner was asking of us. We went through a few iterations asking who was being evaluated in the time to completion (customer order -> customer receiving or barista assigned -> barista delivery) before deciding to ask both.

---

## Part 4 — The Road Not Taken

For the entirety of Category one, I argued that the subsections were functional, as they described system behavior. However, the other members of the group argued that they were non-functional because they believed that they were better attributed to the systems functions rather than descriptive of the function undertaken in the system directly, which I later agreed to.
